Four PSRAM on one Teensy

Every wire, both ends, by pin number. Four ESP-PSRAM64H chips and a 74LVC138A on perfboard, sharing one chip-select line so the processor still believes it is talking to a single 8 MB device.

Read this before you cut anything

The PSRAM already on the Teensy has to stay. The Teensy core probes chip-select 0 first, and if nothing answers there it never probes chip-select 1 at all and reports zero. Your four chips would be perfectly wired and completely invisible. One chip on the underside footprint is what switches the bus on.

Solder the fast six to the underside pads, not to a breadboard. The clock and four data lines need a ground beside them to return along. A breadboard gives them none, and the failure is not a dead board, it is a wrong byte now and then. The decoder and the resistors can go anywhere.

The board

SCLK, one rail SIO0–3, four rails chip select, one per chip 3.3 V ground

Seven signals are shared rails: run them as continuous columns down the perfboard and drop a short stub into each chip. Only pin 1 is different on every chip, and that is the whole point of the decoder.

The four chips, and what you get

Installed
40 MB
Four chips of 8 MB on the perfboard plus the 8 MB already on the Teensy.
Addressable at once
16 MB
The onboard chip is always there. The bank appears as a second 8 MB window, and the three address pins choose which of the four answers inside it.
Speed
unchanged
The decoder sits only in the chip-select path. The clock and data go straight through, so a banked chip runs at the same rate as an onboard one.

Current is not a concern: one chip is selected at a time, so roughly 35 mA active plus about a milliamp of standby across the others. The Teensy's regulator has 250 mA spare.

Three things that will waste your evening

The pulldowns are not optional

The Teensy core probes the bus before your code runs, so whichever bank the address pins happen to select at power-on is the one it finds. Floating pins can select a decoder output with no chip on it, nothing answers, and the core reports 8 MB instead of 16. That looks exactly like a bad solder joint and will send you reflowing a board that was fine. Three 10 k resistors hold bank 0 selected at boot and the problem disappears.

Get the LVC part, not the HC part

The decoder is only in the chip-select path, so whatever it takes to propagate arrives as the chip select being late against a clock that went straight through.

partdelay at 3.3 Vagainst one 88 MHz clock of 11.4 ns
74HC138~30 nsnearly three clocks late
74LVC138A~5 nsunder half a clock

With an HC part the first read of every burst is taken while no chip is listening. If an HC is all you have, the sketch can raise the chip-select setup time to cover it at a cost of about one percent of bandwidth, which is in the code and commented. Halving the bus clock also works and costs 63%.

The cache will lie to you

The upper 8 MB is a window, and whichever bank the address pins select is what appears in it. Switch banks without telling the cache and the processor hands you the previous bank's data, with no error and no warning. Plausible wrong numbers, which is the worst failure there is. The flush lives inside the bank-select function in the sketch so it cannot be forgotten.

Bring-up, in an order that cannot mislead you

  1. Meter the board with no Teensy attached
    3.3 V rail to ground: not a short. Every PSRAM pin 8 to the 3.3 V rail: continuous. Every pin 4 to ground: continuous. Each chip's pin 1 to its own decoder output, and to nothing else.
  2. Power the board alone and check the decoder
    With A, B and C all low, decoder pin 15 should be the only output near 0 V and the rest near 3.3 V. Drive A high by hand and pin 14 should take over.
  3. Attach the Teensy and just look at the reported size
    Run the bank sketch. It prints what the core found before it tests anything. You want 16 MB: 8 onboard, 8 for the bank. Anything less and the list of causes is printed for you, in order of likelihood.
  4. Then run the bank test, which is built to be hard to fool
    It writes every bank before reading any. Writing and reading one bank at a time passes with the decoder disconnected, because the data never leaves cache. Written this way, a dead decoder means all four writes hit the same chip and three banks fail.
