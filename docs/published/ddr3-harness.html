DDR3 harness build sheet

Wiring a DDR3 UDIMM to an Alchitry Cu that bridges it onto a Teensy 4.1's FlexSPI2 bus. Eight data lines reach one x8 chip, so the addressable capacity is 256 MB whatever the label on the stick says. Every wire below has a numbered header pin, and both bitstreams are built.

Read this first: VREF

A DDR3 module will not respond at all without two reference voltages at half of VDD. Every input comparator on every chip measures against them. They are not optional, they are not generated on the module, and nothing else on this page matters if they are missing or wrong.

Power, and the one ordering rule

VDD and VDDQ
1.50 V
Spec window 1.425 to 1.575 V. Size the supply for 1.5 A; expect about 0.5 A drawn. Standby is 32 to 55 mA per chip and there are eight live ones.
VREFDQ and VREFCA
0.750 V
Half of VDD, within 1 percent. That is 7.5 mV of total error budget, so 1 percent resistors, not 5 percent.
Logic side
3.30 V
From the FPGA board. Its banks are fixed at 3.3 V, which is why every one of the 38 lines needs translating.
Bring 1.5 V up first, or at the same instant as 3.3 V

The output lines are resistor dividers fed from 3.3 V logic. If the logic side is live while the module is not, those dividers inject current into unpowered inputs. Wire both regulators to one switch. While you are at it: hold the module's RESET# line low until 1.5 V is settled, which the gateware already does for 200 microseconds, but only once it is itself powered.

VREF: two dividers, built the same way

Two independent dividers, one per reference, each decoupled at the contact it feeds. Do not share one divider between them and do not tap them off a long wire.

PartValueWhy this value
Upper resistor, 1.5 V to the tap100 Ω 1% A 1 k divider sags by 16 mV under the input leakage of sixteen chips, which is 2 percent and out of spec on its own. At 100 Ω the same leakage costs 1.6 mV.

Feed VREFDQ at contact 1 from one divider and VREFCA at contact 67 from the other. Measure both with a meter before the module is ever clocked: anything outside 0.743 to 0.758 V is a fault, not a tolerance.

The 26 output-only lines

Clock, address, bank, command and control only ever travel from the FPGA to the module, so a plain two-resistor divider is the right answer and is better than an active translator here: it is stronger, it has no direction logic to get confused, and it cannot oscillate.

Pick the column that matches the memory clock you are running. The divider's source impedance has to charge roughly 20 pF of module capacitance well inside half a clock period.

The 10 bidirectional lines, and your TXB0108s

The data group turns around: the FPGA drives it on writes and the module drives it on reads. A divider cannot do that, and a 3.3 V input cannot read a 1.5 V high, since it wants 2.0 V to call something a one. These lines need a real translator.

What cfgB needs
31.25 Mb/s
At a 15.625 MHz memory clock. Data moves on both clock edges, so the bit rate is double the clock.
TXB0108 rating
20 Mb/s
At the low end of its supply range, rising to 100 Mb/s at higher rails. Its datasheet also insists any pull resistor exceed 50 kΩ, which tells you the drive is deliberately weak.
At 6.25 MHz instead
12.5 Mb/s
Inside the TXB's rating with margin. This is cfgA, using only modules you already own.

So there are two honest routes, and they are not in conflict:

  • Tonight, with TXB0108s. Flash cfgA.bin and set MEM_KHZ 6250 in the sketch. That is a 6.25 MHz memory clock and the Teensy link on a single data line, and both halves matter: FlexSPI2 cannot be clocked below 49.5 MHz, four lines at that speed consume 24.75 MB/s, and a 6.25 MHz memory supplies only 12.5, so a four-line link here returns rubbish however well the wiring is done. One line takes 6.19 MB/s and leaves a factor of two in hand. Expect it to work and to be slow, about a third of the onboard PSRAM. It proves the wiring, the rails, the references and the initialisation sequence, which is where nearly all the risk lives.
  • For speed, order SN74AVC8T245s. Two of them, direction driven from the FPGA, which already produces the signal. A 74LVC8T245 will not do: its VCCB floor is 1.65 V and the DIMM is 1.5 V. Then flash cfgB.bin and set MEM_KHZ 15625.
The SN74LS245 in your parts bin will not work here

It is a 5 V TTL buffer, not a translator. It has one supply, it cannot output 1.5 V, and its input threshold is 2.0 V so it cannot read 1.5 V either. Keep it for something else.

Every wire, with the header pin you actually solder to

An FPGA ball designator is not a solderable thing. These three columns are what a person holding an iron needs: the numbered header position, the part the wire passes through, and the DIMM contact at the far end. Generated from the pin file the bitstream was built with, so the table and the gateware cannot disagree.

Find pin 1 with a meter before you cut anything

Each Cu bank is 50 positions in two rows of 25, and every third position counting from the end of a row is power or ground: 1, 4, 7 up to 25, then 26, 29, 32 up to 50. That is exactly why the signal numbers below skip. Probe for continuity to board ground and confirm that the positions you believe are 4, 7 and 10 are also power or ground. Getting the row orientation backwards mirrors all 32 signals on that bank, and it is the one mistake on this page that costs a module rather than an afternoon.

Do not wire power and ground from a table

A 240-contact module has roughly thirty VDD and sixty VSS contacts and they must be connected in quantity, not singly. Find them on your own socket with a continuity meter instead of trusting any list: every VSS contact is shorted to every other one on the module, and the same is true of VDD, so two probes and a beeper will map them in a couple of minutes and cannot be out of date.

Decoupling

Sparse decoupling is the most common reason a hand-wired DRAM almost works. Budget for it.

  • 100 nF per VDD contact group, as close to the socket as the leads allow. Aim for at least eight of them spread along the socket rather than one large one at the end.
  • A 10 to 100 µF bulk capacitor at the point the 1.5 V rail arrives at the socket.
  • 100 nF on each translator's 1.5 V pin and 3.3 V pin. These switch hardest and are usually forgotten because the modules look self-contained.
  • 100 nF and 1 µF on each VREF tap, already counted above.

Bring-up, in an order that cannot destroy anything

  1. Read the identity EEPROM with the module unpowered
    Four wires at 3.3 V, nothing else connected. It reports density, row and column counts and the part number. The gateware assumes 8 banks, 15 row bits and 10 column bits; if the EEPROM disagrees, stop and change the gateware rather than the wiring.
    Pass: a plausible part number prints, and the geometry matches.
  2. Build and measure the rails with the module out of the socket
    1.5 V and both 0.75 V references, loaded only by their dividers.
    Pass: 1.425 to 1.575 V, and both references 0.743 to 0.758 V.
  3. Check every divider output with the FPGA pin held high, still with no module
    Tedious and worth it. A divider built with two series resistors instead of a series and a shunt reads 3.3 V and will damage the module the moment it is inserted.
    Pass: all 26 read between 1.4 and 1.6 V when driven high, near 0 V when low.
  4. Confirm the tie-offs with a meter
    Five to ground, one to the rail. Getting S1# and CKE1 the wrong way round leaves the module permanently deselected and looks exactly like a dead part.
    Pass: contacts 195, 77, 169, 125 read 0 V; contact 76 reads 1.5 V.
  5. Insert the module and check current before anything is clocked
    With RESET# low and no clock, the module should be drawing standby current only.
    Pass: roughly 0.1 to 0.5 A. Several amps means a reversed rail; cut power.
  6. Load the gateware at the slow clock and watch the initialisation indicator
    It lights only after the full sequence completes: 200 microseconds of reset, 500 microseconds with the clock enable held low, then the four mode registers in the order the datasheet demands.
    Pass: the indicator lights within about a second of power-up.
  7. Run the memory test from the Teensy
    It writes a walking pattern, flushes the cache, reads it back, then holds for five seconds and reads again so that only refresh can be keeping the data alive.
    Pass: no mismatches, and still none after the hold.
  8. Only then chase speed
    Swap the dividers to the full-speed column, move the memory clock up, and retune the one read-sampling number. Everything before this step stays untouched.
    Pass: the same memory test, at four times the clock.
