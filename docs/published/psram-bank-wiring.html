Four PSRAM on one Teensy

Every wire, both ends, by pin number. Four ESP-PSRAM64H chips and one 74LVC138A on perfboard. Each part is drawn on its own below, full size, with every pin labelled with exactly what goes on it.

Read this before you cut anything

The PSRAM already on the Teensy has to stay. The Teensy's startup code checks chip-select 0 first. If nothing answers there, it never checks chip-select 1 at all and reports zero. Your four chips would be perfectly wired and completely invisible. One chip on the underside footprint is what switches the whole bus on.

Solder the six fast wires to the Teensy's underside pads. Not to a breadboard. The clock and the four data lines need a ground running beside them to come back along. A breadboard gives them none, and the failure is not a dead board, it is a wrong byte now and then. The decoder and the resistors can sit anywhere.

The whole board

This is the thing itself. The Teensy on the left, the decoder under it, the four chips in a column on the right. Seven lanes run down the board and every chip taps every one of them — that is how the chips connect to each other. Only pin 1 gets its own wire, and that is the entire reason the decoder exists.

Read it like this. Start at a Teensy pad on the left. Follow its wire up and across to the top of a lane. Follow the lane down. Every chip it passes has a dot on it where that chip's pin joins. One lane, four chips, one signal. Then do the same for the next pad.

The four violet wires are the odd ones out. Each starts at a different decoder output and ends at exactly one chip's pin 1. Those four wires are the only thing that differs between the chips.

1Close up: the Teensy

Six wires come off the underside pads and five off the ordinary top pins. Pad 48 you do not touch — it is the chip that has to stay.

So each chip takes seven identical wires plus one capacitor plus one wire nobody else has. Do one chip completely, check it against the drawing, then repeat three times.

The onboard chip is always there. The bank is a second 8 MB window, and the address pins choose which of the four appears in it.
Speed
unchanged
The decoder is only in the chip-select path. Clock and data go straight through, so a banked chip runs as fast as an onboard one.

Three things that will waste your evening

The three pulldowns are not optional

The Teensy checks the bus before your code runs, so whichever bank the address pins happen to select at power-on is the one it finds. Floating pins can select an output with no chip on it, nothing answers, and it reports 8 MB instead of 16. That looks exactly like a bad solder joint and will send you reflowing a board that was fine. Three 10 k resistors hold bank 0 selected at boot.

Get the LVC part, not the HC part

The decoder sits only in the chip-select path, so whatever it takes to propagate shows up as the chip select arriving late against a clock that went straight through.

partdelay at 3.3 Vagainst one clock of 11.4 ns
74HC138~30 nsnearly three clocks late
74LVC138A~5 nsunder half a clock

With an HC part the first read of every burst happens while no chip is listening. If an HC is all you have, the sketch can stretch the chip-select setup time to cover it for about one percent of bandwidth. That is in the code and commented.

The cache will lie to you

The upper 8 MB is a window, and whichever bank the address pins select is what appears in it. Switch banks without telling the cache and the processor hands you the previous bank's data, with no error and no warning. Plausible wrong numbers, which is the worst failure there is. The flush lives inside the bank-select function so it cannot be forgotten.

Bring-up, in an order that cannot mislead you

  1. Meter the board with the Teensy unplugged
    3.3 V rail to ground: not a short. Every PSRAM pin 8 to the 3.3 V rail: continuous. Every pin 4 to ground: continuous. Each chip's pin 1 to its own decoder output, and to nothing else.
  2. Power the board on its own and watch the decoder
    With A, B and C all low, pin 15 should be the only output near 0 V and the rest near 3.3 V. Pull A high by hand and pin 14 takes over.
  3. Plug in the Teensy and just read the reported size
    Run the bank sketch. It prints what was found before it tests anything. You want 16 MB: 8 onboard and 8 for the bank. Anything less and it prints the likely causes in order.
  4. Then run the bank test, which is built to be hard to fool
    It writes every bank before reading any. Writing and reading one bank at a time passes even with the decoder disconnected, because the data never leaves cache. Done this way, a dead decoder means all four writes hit the same chip and three banks fail.
