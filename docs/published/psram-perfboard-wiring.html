Why the enable must be active low, and how that is known rather than guessed.

On the breadboard the enable is driven by a chip select, which is active low, and all six chips do answer. Had it been on the active-high G1 input, asserting the select would have disabled the decoder and nothing behind it could ever have replied. So it goes to G2A, G1 is tied high, and G2B is grounded.

The part that decides whether it was worth building

Everything above is connectivity and it will work. The reason to leave the breadboard is the ground return, and it is the one thing a breadboard cannot give.

It was never a software problem, and it is not the pad drive either. A sweep of drive strength, slew rate and input hysteresis — eight combinations — gave the same 14.53 MB/s every time. The wiring is the limit, measured rather than assumed.

In order of how much each is worth

  1. A ground wire beside the five bus signals, the whole length of the chain, bonded to Teensy ground at both ends. Every switching data line needs a return path. On a breadboard the return goes the long way through the power rails, and that loop is what sets 40 ns. This is the change that can buy the factor of two.
  2. A chain, not a star. One run past each chip in turn, each chip's stub under about 15 mm. A star makes every branch a reflection.
  3. Shortest total run to the furthest chip. Close to linear, so every centimetre counts.
  4. 10 µF bulk at the 3.3 V entry to the group, and another at the far end. The 100 nF at each chip handles the fast edge; the bulk handles the group. Worth fitting, and not what limits the speed: at a failing edge rate, changing burst length from 4 to 32 bytes and adding idle gaps from 0 to 50 µs moved the error count only between 666 and 691 of 1024. Charge is not the problem. Signal return is.

Even short of 20.2 ns, nanoseconds are linear throughput. Going from 40 to 30 ns is a third more read bandwidth with no other change. There is no cliff to clear before the work pays.

Flash this when it is done

One sketch. It assumes nothing about what got soldered, and it has already been run end to end against the existing breadboard, so its own logic is not in question.

firmware/bench-one/tests/psram_perfboard/

Six stages, then it keeps going
stagewhat it answers
1which of the ten possible chip selects has a chip on it, by identity command at 250 kHz
2are those banks separate chips, or does one answer on several selects
3each bank's own clean timing window, and nanoseconds per nibble against 20.2
4chip select low per burst against the 8 µs refresh limit, and the burst chosen to fit it

6then soaks every bank indefinitely, printing the error-rate bound as it falls

What to look at, in order

  • Stage 1 should list eight chips. If no decoder bank answers, the enable wire is the suspect and the sketch says so by name. If nothing at all answers, it prints the four likely causes in order, with pin 53 first.
  • Stage 3's ns/nibble column is the number this board exists for. Under 20.2 and the hardware controller becomes reachable, which brings memory mapping and about 33 MB/s. Then run psram_clock_sweep.
  • Stage 4 choosing a burst shorter than 96 bytes is correct, not a fault. Timing margin and refresh margin trade against each other: stepping one setting slower for timing lengthens a burst, and on the breadboard that took chip select low from 6.93 µs to 7.71 and produced one wrong byte in 8 MB. Shortening the burst to 80 gave 6.52 µs and zero. The sketch does that arithmetic per bank.
  • Stage 6's bound is the qualification. A bank with no errors yet is bounded, not proven. Leave it running. On the breadboard the bound reached 0.7 per billion after 1.3 GB per configuration.

What 64 megabytes changes

Capacity is not speed, and it decides something speed does not: whether a model has to be split across boards at all.

A 24-layer model at hidden size 512 is 55 MB of weights. That fits on one Teensy with room over. The pipeline, the hop cost, the vocabulary split and the load balancing all exist because models did not fit on one node — and for this size, after this board, they do. Distribution becomes a throughput choice rather than a necessity.

At hidden size 1024 a layer is 9.2 MB, so six fit and splitting is still required. The boundary moves; it does not vanish.

One thing the controller still cannot do. If the board gets under 20.2 ns, FlexSPI2 becomes usable — for the chip on pad 48 only.

The controller asserts its own chip select as part of a transfer and it has exactly two, pads 48 and 51. Pad 51 is the one that tore off. So it can reach onboard chip A and nothing else, while the eight decoder banks stay on the hand-driven driver. Making it reach all eight would mean putting the enable on pad 48 and lifting chip A's pin 1 off it — the upgrade path, not the build.

So the honest expectation is 8 MB at controller speed and 56 MB at bit-bang speed, with the bit-bang speed itself improved by whatever the ground return buys.

Every figure here was measured on a Teensy 4.1 with six ESP-PSRAM64H, never on a desktop. The breadboard this replaces verified 8,388,608 bytes with zero errors at 21.31 MB/s write and 13.80 read, and soaked 7.9 GB without a single wrong byte. Full record in docs/40 through docs/47.
