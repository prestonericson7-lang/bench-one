01

What the fabric is for

The fabric is a nervous system, not a brain. It routes, expands and addresses; it does not compute. Every chip in it exists to turn a small number of scarce microcontroller pins into a large number of cheap, named, individually addressable ones.

The rule that makes it safe is that Teensy 1 is the only device wired to the SPI and I2C buses. The Luckfox, the radio and the display node ask for IO over their UARTs and Teensy 1 performs it. That is not a convention anyone has to remember — the other boards have no wire to those buses at all, so a second bus master is impossible by construction.

The minimal core is five chips

One 74HC138 gives eight chip selects. Two MCP23017 give 32 interrupt-capable IO on two wires. One 74HC595 becomes the fabric's own control byte. One 74HC165 gives eight snapshot inputs. One 74HC373 makes the shared bus safe. Everything after that is more of the same, and none of it needs another Teensy pin.

02

The five nodes, and which wires carry what

The display connector that looks right and is not

The E32R40T link uses P4, silkscreened I2C — not P2, silkscreened UART. P2 is UART0 wired in parallel with the onboard CH340C through 100 Ω series resistors: with USB plugged in, a Teensy pulling low only reaches about 2.2 V at the ESP32's input, which is still a logic high. The link looks completely dead, only when USB is connected, with nothing wrong anywhere you would think to look. P2 pin 1 is also a bidirectional 5 V node through a MOSFET body diode. The connectors are 1.25 mm pitch; JST-PH 2.0 mm will not mate.

03

The Teensy 1 pin budget

Three assignments were fixed before anything else. Serial1 on pins 0/1 is the tested radio link. Wire on 18/19 is the only LPI2C1 pin pair the chip has. SPI2 is unreachable because its pins are the microSD socket's.

That leaves SPI0 and SPI1, and the fabric gets its own. SPI1's default MISO is pin 1 — the radio's TX — but it has an alternate on pin 39, selected with SPI1.setMISO(39) before begin(). Miss that call and the radio link goes silent with no error anywhere.

04

Chip select: four pins, eight modules, and the glitch that must not happen

A 74HC138 decodes three address bits into eight mutually exclusive active-low outputs. It has no "select nothing" address — all eight codes assert something — so pin 6 (E3, active high) is dedicated as a global deselect. That is the only way to reach a state where no module is selected.

The subtle part is what happens when the address changes. The three address lines are three separate register writes, tens of nanoseconds apart. If the decoder is enabled while they change, the active output walks across whatever intermediate addresses those partial writes spell.

Which vendor you read decides what you can guarantee

Neither TI (SCLS107G) nor Nexperia tabulates a single AC parameter for the 74HC138 at 3.3 V — their columns are 2.0, 4.5 and 6.0 V. onsemi publishes a full guaranteed 3.0 V column for the MC74HC138A, and since 3.3 V is above 3.0 V and HC delay falls monotonically with supply, that column is a valid and much tighter bound.

05

The shift chain, and the part you do not own

A 74HC595 chain turns MOSI into as many outputs as you have chips. A 74HC165 chain turns MISO into as many inputs. Both are SPI mode 0, so one clock serves both.

The problem is that a 74HC165's output is a permanently-driving totem pole. It has no chip select and no tri-state. The moment it is wired to MISO it fights anything else that drives that line — an estimated 17 to 40 mA per conflicting bit, against a 25 mA per-pin absolute maximum. That is a damage problem, not a data problem.

The usual fix is a 74HC125 tri-state buffer. You do not have one. You have fifteen 74HC373, and tying its LE permanently high turns it into exactly that.

The byte-order trap

MOSI feeds chip 0's serial input, and chip 0's QH′ feeds chip 1. So the first byte sent is pushed all the way along and comes to rest in the farthest chip. To make data[0] land in the chip nearest the Teensy, it must be transmitted last.

Getting this backwards does not fail. It works perfectly, with the chips in the wrong order, and presents as mirrored wiring.

The bit-order trap

A '165 shifts out stage 8 first, which is loaded from pin 6. So pin 6 (H) arrives as bit 7 and pin 11 (A) as bit 0. A bank wired A through H in ascending physical order reads back mirrored.

Also mirrored-looking, also not a wiring fault. Walk a single 1 through the chain to map it once and write the map down.

Never tie RCLK to SRCLK

The storage latch is what makes a '595 bank atomic: the shift register clocks through every intermediate state while the outputs hold the previous value, and all of them change on one RCLK edge. Tying the two clocks together — a common shortcut — puts every intermediate shift state onto the pins. On a relay or MOSFET bank that means briefly energising loads that were never commanded.

06

Sixty-four analog channels for one pin

Eight 74HC4051, all Z outputs bussed to a single Teensy ADC pin. Conventionally this costs seven GPIO: three select lines, three leaf-address lines and an enable. Here it costs zero, because those seven signals live in a byte that is already being shifted out — chip 0 of the '595 chain, reserved as the fabric's control byte.

The price is honest: changing a channel now costs one SPI transaction and an RCLK pulse, roughly a microsecond, instead of three near-instant pin writes. That is irrelevant, because the mux needs 5 to 20 µs of settling before its output is trustworthy anyway. The mux is the slow part. Nothing was lost.

Not one 3.3 V number for this part exists

Neither Nexperia nor TI characterises the 74HC4051 at 3.3 V at all. On-resistance, turn-on and turn-off are specified only at 2.0, 4.5, 6.0 and 9.0 V. Every "70 Ω at 3.3 V" figure in circulation is somebody's measurement of their own parts, usually derived from the 4.5 V column and three to four times optimistic.

The firmware designs against the 2.0 V bound — Ron 300 Ω, ton 350 ns — because that is the only guaranteed envelope that contains 3.3 V. Charge injection is not specified by either vendor either; the closest published proxy is Nexperia's control-to-switch crosstalk of 110 mV peak-to-peak at 4.5 V.

The bus sets the settling time, not the converter

Bussing eight Z pins puts roughly 200 pF on the ADC node (8 × ~25 pF Ccom). The RT1062's sample capacitor is about 2 pF — a hundred times smaller. Teensy's stock 12-bit configuration already gives a 2.4 µs sample aperture, 18× more than the converter itself needs, so the entire timing budget is bus settling.

With a low-impedance source that is under a microsecond. With a 10 kΩ sensor it is τ = 2.06 µs and about 18.5 µs to half an LSB, which is what the 20 µs wait is sized for.

A consequence worth knowing before using a leaf for anything AC: the datasheet's 170–180 MHz bandwidth is measured into 50 Ω. Your bussed node is roughly 250 Ω into 250 pF, which is about 2.5 MHz — seventy times lower.

Why the first reading is always thrown away

The sample capacitor arrives still holding the previous channel's voltage and charge-shares with the bus. The error is about 0.79% of the step — up to roughly 32 LSB at 12 bits — and it is proportional to the previous channel. Keep that sample and every reading becomes a small function of its neighbour: stable, repeatable, plausible and wrong.

One discarded conversion removes it entirely, because the second one samples a node the first already charged. The driver discards unconditionally and counts how many.

Break-before-make applies here too, and for a different reason

The '4051's own break-before-make guarantee covers channels within one package. It says nothing about two different packages — and that is exactly the overlap here. While the leaf decoder's address settles, two of its outputs can be low simultaneously for a few nanoseconds, connecting two analog sources through about 500 Ω.

Latching the whole control byte on one RCLK edge already makes the address change simultaneous, which beats three separate GPIO writes. The firmware still clears the enable bit in its own latched write first, exactly as the chip-select path does. Two SPI transactions instead of one, for about a microsecond.

Float VEE and nothing looks wrong

Leave pin 7 unconnected and the part still appears to work. The internal level shifter limps along on leakage, then Ron drifts into the kilohms and readings become a slowly-moving mid-scale number that tracks whichever channel you read previously. It never rails, never zeroes, and never throws an error. Tie VEE to GND on every leaf.

And never to a negative rail, whatever the datasheet's ±5 V examples suggest: with VEE below ground the analog range extends below the ADC's 0 V floor, and any negative excursion forward-biases the Teensy's own pin protection.

07

The expander bank and its one silent killer

Eight MCP23017 on two wires give 128 interrupt-capable IO. Each is strapped to an address from 0x20 to 0x27 by three pins that are TTL buffers with no internal bias — left floating they are not "low", they are undefined, and the chip answers at an address that can change with temperature or with a hand near the board.

All eight interrupt outputs are configured open-drain and wire-OR onto a single Teensy pin with one pull-up. Any expander with something to say pulls the line low; the service routine reads each chip's interrupt-flag register to find out which.

The failure that stops interrupts with no error anywhere

With MIRROR = 1, the interrupt condition does not clear until both GPIOA and GPIOB have been read. A service routine that reads only the port it cared about leaves the line asserted forever. No further falling edge ever arrives, interrupts stop system-wide, and nothing reports anything.

The driver reads all sixteen bits in one transaction so that "both" cannot be got wrong, and raises IRQ_STORM if the line is still low afterwards. It also reads INTCAP before GPIO: INTCAP holds the pin values at the instant the interrupt fired, and GPIO holds them now. Reading GPIO alone turns a 2 ms button press into no event at all if the service came 3 ms later.

Sink, never source

The MCP23017 guarantees its output levels at 8 mA sinking but only 3 mA sourcing, with package limits of 150 mA out of VSS and 125 mA into VDD. So every LED on this fabric goes anode to 3.3 V through its resistor, with the expander pulling the cathode low. The internal pull-ups are 40–115 µA, roughly 100 kΩ — fine for a switch on the same board, far too weak for anything on a cable.

Why the 74LS245 is not a "close enough" transceiver

At 3.3 V its Darlington totem-pole output cannot reach the 2.31 V (0.7 × 3.3 V) input-high threshold that both 74HC parts and the i.MX RT1062 require — so the bus never reads a valid high. Run it at 5 V instead and its output high, typically 3.4 V with no maximum specified, sits at or above the Teensy's 3.6 V absolute maximum. Both ends of the trade are broken. Buy an SN74HC245N, or an SN74LVC245AN if any 5 V signal will ever touch the bus.

The bottleneck is not where the bulk order suggests

The shift registers are abundant — 24 × '595, 24 × '138, 19 × '165, 15 × '373. What you are short of is glue: four each of 74HC00, 08, 14, 32 and 164 across both kits combined, not four per kit.

That matters the moment a second stack exists. Stack 2 needs the same glue, and there is not enough for two. It also means the 74HC14 — the correct part for every debounce and every slow edge in the build — is one of the scarcest things in the box.

Never use a 74HC164 on a chip select

A '164 has no storage latch, so every intermediate shift state appears on its outputs. Driving a chip select or a latch enable from one means asserting a series of wrong selects on the way to the right one — the same failure as tying RCLK to SRCLK, with the same consequence. It is fine for an LED bar, where nobody minds the pattern flickering during a shift.

Bring-up, in the order that isolates faults

Each rung assumes the ones below it passed. Running them out of order does not just waste time — it produces the wrong diagnosis, because a fault at a lower layer presents as a fault at a higher one.

Two tests report SKIPPED, and that is the honest answer

Chip-select exclusivity and global deselect are the two most important things the fabric does, and nothing inside the Teensy can read the 74HC138's outputs back. The self-test walks all eight addresses and pulses the marker pin so the analyser can prove it, then reports SKIPPED rather than PASS. A self-test that passes by not looking is worse than no self-test, because it gets trusted. Capture them once and they become real passes with a golden trace behind them.

Instrument limits, stated so a capture is not misread

A 24 MHz 8-channel analyser gives roughly 8 MSa/s per channel with all eight in use, so SPI above about 4 MHz cannot be honestly decoded while capturing. There is a separate 2 MHz capture clock in the firmware for exactly this. Measuring at one speed and shipping at another without saying so is how a verified timing claim quietly becomes fiction.

On I2C, expect to measure below 400 kHz: the Teensy's peripheral adds latency proportional to SCL rise time, so 250 ns of rise gives about 364 kHz and 500 ns gives about 333 kHz. A capture showing 360 kHz is healthy. One showing 286 kHz means roughly a microsecond of rise time, and the pull-ups want to be stronger before more devices go on that bus.

10

Rules that are not negotiable

  • No floating CMOS input, ever. Tie every unused 74HC input, every /OE, every /MR and every /SRCLR. A floating input oscillates around the switching threshold and turns on both halves of the output stage at once. This is the single most common cause of "random" behaviour and unexplained supply current.
  • Teensy 4.1 pins are 3.3 V only and its absolute maximum is 3.6 V. Nothing 5 V reaches a pin without a shifter in front of it.
  • Pulled buses get a TXS0108, push-pull gets a TXB0108. Never a TXB on I2C — its internal one-shots fight the pull-ups. Keep translated stubs under 10 cm and pull /OE to the low-side supply.
  • Decouple every chip. 100 nF X7R within 10 mm of the supply pin, 10 µF per section, 220–470 µF at the ESP32 for transmit brownout. Star ground at the rail entry.
  • Cut the VUSB–VIN pad on both Teensys before powering them from an external 5 V rail with USB attached.
  • Two /OE low at once is a short through two output stages. Derive every /OE from one decoder so exclusivity is guaranteed by the function table, and add 100–220 Ω series resistors to survive the nanoseconds of overlap that remain.
