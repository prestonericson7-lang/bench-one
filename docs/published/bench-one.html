The newest result: DDR3 on a microcontroller

A Teensy 4.1 is rated for 16 MB of external RAM. A DDR3 stick from a dead laptop holds 4 GB and costs nothing. Putting a small FPGA between them — one that pretends to be a PSRAM chip, so the memory appears as ordinary pointers with no driver in the read path — gives that microcontroller 256 MB using 16% of a $30 FPGA.

The FPGA performs no arithmetic, deliberately. It moves bytes and nothing else, so the throughput that comes out is the microcontroller's own number rather than a property of the pair.

The interesting part is not the speed. It is what the exercise revealed about where the limit actually sits.

Effective throughput in MB/s, which is what predicts tokens per second. Reading and unpacking add on a CPU; only fabric overlaps them. That one rule reproduces the measured PSRAM figure exactly, which is why it is trusted for the rest. The one-line link is the configuration buildable with ordinary level translators; the four-line one needs better parts and is still 0.84x the PSRAM it replaces. Neither is faster than what it displaces. Both are 32 times the capacity, which is the entire case for building them.

There is deliberately no row between those two. A middle configuration looks obvious and cannot be built: the peripheral that consumes the data cannot be clocked below 49.5 MHz, which on four lines eats 24.75 MB/s, and the rate rule then demands 28.75 MB/s of memory behind it. The way out is a narrower link rather than a slower one.

Four ceilings stack, and the one that binds was the last to be found. The processor's external bus tops out at 66.5 MB/s, a property of the package rather than a tuning target: the three faster paths that exist on the silicon are each closed by pins that were never bonded out. The unpacking wall sits at 39.3, well below that. And below both sits the FPGA fabric, which turned out to be the real limit.

That one only appeared once place-and-route could actually run. The gateware does not close at the board's 100 MHz oscillator: across eight placer seeds its maximum frequency lands between 81.5 and 97.4 MHz, so the luckiest seed very nearly makes it. A design that passes because of a lucky seed has not passed. The fabric now runs off its PLL at 50 or 62.5 MHz instead, the memory clock is that divided by at least four, and 15.625 MHz is the fastest DDR3 clock this board can actually hold. Every build now routes on four seeds and fails if any one of them misses.

Above roughly 66 MB/s of raw read, more memory bandwidth buys literally nothing anyway. The fabric ceiling costs less than it looks like it should, because the link was already the limit long before the DRAM was.

This project had treated memory as the scarce resource throughout. For a single microcontroller, it is not. That reframes where the remaining effort belongs.

Why DDR3 at 15 MHz is legal, not a hack

Everyone assumes DDR3 needs hundreds of megahertz. It has a documented DLL disable mode, and the datasheet specifies the clock period from 8 ns to 7800 ns — 125 MHz down to 128 kHz. Running at 15.625 MHz, or at 6.25, sits comfortably inside a published window. The DIMM is the one part of this design with bandwidth to spare.

Every timing quoted in nanoseconds is a minimum, so a slow clock satisfies them in one or two cycles and the numbers that make DDR3 hard stop applying. One does not relax: refresh is a wall-clock deadline of 8192 commands every 64 ms, and it is the only thing that bounds how long a single transfer may be.

The catch that shapes the whole design. With the DLL disabled the datasheet states that the strobe the chip returns "could be larger than tCK" — it can arrive more than a whole clock late — and then declines to bound it. So read data is not latched on the strobe at all. It is sampled at a fixed offset, and that offset is the one number in the design that cannot be calculated, only found on the bench.

Which is why it is settable at runtime. As a build-time constant, every wrong guess cost a synthesis, a place-and-route and a reflash. As a runtime value, the microcontroller sweeps the whole space in about a second and prints which settings return data intact, choosing the middle of the widest clean run so there is margin on both sides:

Four faults worth knowing if you build anything like this

Each was found by building a model that argues back, and each would have been real on silicon.

  • Read latency is the datasheet figure plus one. The datasheet measures from the edge where the device latches a command. Registering commands on the falling edge to buy setup time means the device sees them a clock later. Using the published number samples one clock early and returns the previous burst's last two bytes in front of every word — a very convincing wrong answer.
  • A ready signal must mean "accepting now", not "available". Requests are latched only on a memory clock edge, one fabric cycle in four. An unqualified ready is true for three cycles where nothing can be accepted, and a requester that drops its valid on seeing ready loses three requests in four. The failure is silent and total.
  • A flip-flop has one asynchronous reset. Listing both chip select and reset as edge events is legal Verilog and unsynthesisable.
  • Cross clock domains with a toggle, not a pulse. A single-cycle pulse between unrelated clocks can be missed or latched metastable. Here the symptom would have been a calibration sweep that occasionally lies — sending someone hunting hardware that is fine.

The honest headline

Not "cheap GPU". This hardware loses general compute badly and will keep losing it.

What it has is the time domain, where it wins by roughly four orders of magnitude and cannot be bought out of it, and capacity per dollar at the edge of what is physically possible. The constraint that turns out to bound the whole machine is neither compute nor bandwidth but DRAM refresh power — which makes the enclosure a radiator and sets how large the thing can grow before it cooks itself.

That was not what anyone set out to look for, and it is the most interesting thing in the repository.
